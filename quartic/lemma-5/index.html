1. Introduction

In the query model an algorithm computes a function f ⁣:{0,1}N→{0,1}f\colon\{0,1\}^N\to\{0,1\}f:{0,1}N→{0,1} by reading input bits, and we charge one unit for each bit it reads. We write R(f)R(f)R(f) and Q(f)Q(f)Q(f) for the bounded-error randomized and quantum query complexities of fff. For total functions these measures are polynomially related, and the natural question is how large the largest polynomial gap can be. Following Aaronson, Ben-David and Kothari (Aaronson et al. 2016, sec. 2.3), the critical exponent of RRR relative to QQQ is the infimum of the exponents rrr such that R(f)=O(Q(f)r)R(f)=O(Q(f)^r)R(f)=O(Q(f)r) holds for all total fff.

Grover’s algorithm (Grover 1996) gives a quadratic gap for the OR function, and for many years this was believed to be the largest gap for total functions. Aaronson, Ben-David and Kothari (Aaronson et al. 2016) refuted this with the cheat-sheet technique and a power 2.52.52.5 separation, up to logarithmic factors. Improvements in the underlying partial-function separations then gave power 8/3−o(1)8/3-o(1)8/3−o(1) by Tal (Tal 2020), and power 3−o(1)3-o(1)3−o(1) by Bansal and Sinha (Bansal and Sinha 2021) and, independently, Sherstov, Storozhenko and Wu (Sherstov et al. 2023). On the other side, Aaronson, Ben-David, Kothari, Rao and Tal (Aaronson et al. 2021) used Huang’s sensitivity theorem (Huang 2019) to prove D(f)=O(Q(f)4)D(f)=O(Q(f)^4)D(f)=O(Q(f)4), and conjectured that R(f)=O(Q(f)3)R(f)=O(Q(f)^3)R(f)=O(Q(f)3) (Aaronson et al. 2021, Conjecture 2). 1.1 Overview of techniques

The input consists of mmm columns. Column iii holds an mmm-bit string XiX_iXi​, a few address inputs, and an array of cells. The function asks whether some column wins: its string is all ones, and its cell at the address computed from its address inputs contains a correct certificate pointing to a zero in every other string. At most one column can win. Pointers from a column to zeros in other columns go back to the pointer functions of Göös, Pitassi and Watson (Göös et al. 2018) and of Ambainis et al. (Ambainis et al. 2017), and cells addressed by answers to partial-function instances to the cheat sheets of (Aaronson et al. 2016).

The cost of plain search. Checking that a given column wins costs about m\sqrt mm​ quantum queries, by Grover search over its roughly mmm local tests. One way to find a winner would be to search over the mmm columns with this check inside the search. But then the costs multiply to m⋅m=m\sqrt m\cdot\sqrt m=mm​⋅m​=m, the cost of balanced AND–OR cheat sheets built from the same partial function, whose bounds O~(m)\widetilde O(m)O(m) and Ω~(m3−1/k)\widetilde\Omega(m^{3-1/k})Ω(m3−1/k) give power 3−1/k3-1/k3−1/k (Aaronson et al. 2016, sec. 3). The new construction instead lists a few candidates with cheap comparisons and verifies only those, so the two costs add to O~k(m)\widetilde O_k(\sqrt m)Ok​(m​). The classical bound drops to m2−1/km^{2-1/k}m2−1/k, and in powers of m\sqrt mm​ the exponent rises from 3−1/k3-1/k3−1/k to 4−2/k4-2/k4−2/k.

Why the pointers point outward. The winner’s certificate stores, for every other column, the row of a zero in that column’s string. A comparison between two columns reads one pointer in each direction. If the winner’s address is decoded correctly, its pointer finds a zero in the rival, while no rival pointer can find a zero in the winner’s all-one string. So the winner is selected with high probability even when the rival’s address inputs violate their promise.

Comparisons with arbitrary outcome probabilities. A loser’s address may be undefined, and its decoder can then return any distribution over addresses. So the probability that one loser beats another can lie anywhere in [0,1][0,1][0,1], with no gap from 1/21/21/2, and majority voting cannot turn these comparisons into the fixed tournament that quantum minimum finding (Dürr and Høyer 1996) or king finding (Mande et al. 2023) would need. OpenAI’s tournament instead samples pivots with weights equal to the probability of beating all earlier pivots; Section 4 derives this rule from the requirement that every pairwise bias cancel.

The classical bound. The hard distributions differ only in one hidden zero and one cell of a uniformly random column. A decision tree notices the difference only if it finds that zero, invests many queries in that column’s address inputs, or queries the correct cell while investing few. The last event is a correct prediction of rrr independent hard values, because the index of a cell query already names its address; a direct-product theorem with per-input caps bounds it, and a capped simulation avoids conditioning on which columns the tree investigates.

Ben-David and Kothari (Ben-David and Kothari 2026) and Ambainis, Iraids and Kokainis (Ambainis et al. 2026) also compare candidate witnesses, in separations of certificate complexity from randomized and quantum query complexity.
