## 1. The SK model and the Parisi formula

<!-- passage:sec-1 -->

Each unordered pair contributes one Gaussian coefficient. In (1.1), the factor $-\beta/\sqrt N$ multiplies the sum over $i<j$.

Talagrand treats the energies of all configurations as a centred Gaussian family. Its covariance determines the law of that family.

In (1.3), the covariance divided by $N$ differs from $\xi(R)$ by at most $c(N)$, uniformly in both configurations, with $c(N)\to0$.

The overlap $R(\sigma,\tau)=N^{-1}\sum_i\sigma_i\tau_i$ is the proportion of agreements minus the proportion of disagreements.

For two centred Gaussian Hamiltonians with the same limiting covariance, the triangle inequality bounds the difference of their normalized covariance matrices by the sum of their errors in (1.3).

The level masses $m_\ell$ are nondecreasing, and $m_\ell$ is the weight assigned to the interval between $q_\ell$ and $q_{\ell+1}$.

Starting from the terminal $\log\cosh$ term, integrate the Gaussian increments one level at a time. At mass zero the recursion takes an ordinary expectation.

The finite-level variational value adds $\log 2$ and the output of the recursion, then subtracts half the mass-weighted increments of $\theta(q)=q\xi'(q)-\xi(q)$.

The infimum ranges over the number of levels and all admissible masses and breakpoints at each level.

**Theorem 1.1. The Parisi formula.** The limiting expected log partition function per spin equals the infimum of the finite-level Parisi functionals.

Adding one centred Gaussian to every configuration changes the sample log partition function by that Gaussian. Its expectation stays fixed.

## 2. Guerra’s interpolation and overlap estimates

<!-- passage:sec-2 -->

Guerra’s interpolation weights the original disorder by $\sqrt t$ and the auxiliary Gaussian fields by $\sqrt{1-t}$.

First sum the exponentials of the interpolating energies over spin configurations. Then take the logarithm before applying the Gaussian recursion.

The auxiliary fields are integrated from the innermost level outwards, using the mass of each level in its logarithmic average.

After the auxiliary fields have been integrated, average over the original disorder and divide by $N$ to obtain $\varphi(t)$.

At level $\ell$, a tilted two-replica law measures the overlap of two configurations sampled with the specified sharing of Gaussian fields.

**Theorem 2.1. Guerra’s derivative formula.** Guerra’s identity gives $\varphi'(t)$ as a deterministic correction and a replica remainder, with an error controlled by $c(N)$ under (1.3).

Convexity gives $\xi(R)-\xi(q)-\xi'(q)(R-q)\ge0$. Guerra’s derivative subtracts a weighted average of these terms.

At $t=0$ the original disorder vanishes. The partition sum factors over sites and the resulting pressure is given by the scalar recursion.

**Equation (2.15). Guerra’s upper bound.** Integrate the derivative bound from zero to one and insert the decoupled value at zero. The result is a finite-size upper bound for every admissible scheme.

**Theorem 2.2. Convergence below time one.** Choose $t_0<1$ first. A fixed-level minimizer sufficiently close to the unrestricted infimum gives convergence to the affine comparison at each $t\in[0,t_0]$.

The affine pressure $\psi(t)$ starts at the decoupled pressure and decreases at the deterministic rate in Guerra’s identity.

For a fixed level count, the allowed masses and breakpoints form a compact set. The finite-level functional is continuous on it.

When two adjacent variable breakpoints coincide, the Gaussian step between them has variance zero. Lean deletes that level while preserving the functional and the interpolated pressure. The first or last variable breakpoint may still coincide with its fixed endpoint.

Adjacent Gaussian averages with the same mass compose to one average with the sum of their variances.

At time one, the interpolated pressure is the SK pressure, while the affine comparison is the value of the chosen finite scheme. To compare them, choose a time below one, then take the limit in system size for a suitable scheme. The derivative bound is uniform in system size and controls the remaining distance to the endpoint.

The tail in (2.20) starts where the squared deviation from the target breakpoint is at least a multiple of the current pressure gap $\psi(t)-\varphi(t)$ plus a tolerance $\varepsilon_1$. For sufficiently large $N$, its replica probability is at most $\varepsilon_1$ throughout the time interval.

Split the overlap remainder at the moving threshold in (2.20). The threshold bounds the squared deviations below it, and the tail bound controls the contribution on the tail. The resulting differential inequality (2.22) can be integrated from the common value of the two pressures at time zero.

For the shared fields of the two-replica construction, halve the masses up to the split. The recursion then agrees with twice the one-replica recursion when the overlap constraint is removed.

The constrained pressure comes from a sum over pairs whose overlap is exactly $u$. The two replicas use the same original disorder.

**Theorem 2.4. Quadratic bound on constrained pressure.** The constrained pressure lies below $2\psi(t)$ by a quadratic penalty in the distance from the target overlap, with an additive covariance error of $2c(N)$.

**Proposition 2.5. Exponential bound on overlap probability.** A fixed positive deficit below $2\varphi(t)$ in the constrained pressure makes the event of a prescribed overlap exponentially unlikely in $N$.

If $j$ of the $N$ coordinates agree, then $R=(2j-N)/N$. There are at most $N+1$ values to sum over.

Gaussian concentration controls the event that the constrained partition sum is much larger than its mean-pressure estimate. The doubling identities transfer the event bound to the level replica law.

## 3. A second interpolation for two replicas

<!-- passage:sec-3 -->

The second interpolation varies a new parameter while the first interpolation time $t$ stays fixed. Its derivative is bounded by the deterministic covariance increments.

At the zero endpoint of the second interpolation, sites decouple. The resulting scalar two-replica recursion can be compared with twice the one-replica value.

The two replicas in each summand have overlap $R_{1,2}=u$. Holding that overlap fixed makes the cross terms from the two copies easier to bound.

## 4. Gaussian operators and variational estimates

<!-- passage:sec-4 -->

The operator in (4.1) adds a Gaussian of variance $v$. At positive mass $m$ it applies $m^{-1}\log\mathbb E\exp(m\,\cdot)$, and at zero mass it takes an ordinary expectation.

Differentiating in the Gaussian variance gives the heat term and the mass times the square of the spatial derivative.

Keep the total variance fixed and vary how it is split between two levels. The sign is controlled by the order of their masses.

Insert a level and vary its mass starting at the preceding mass. Near optimality prevents the first variation from being strongly negative.

At a minimizing breakpoint, the first variation in its location gives a stationarity identity. The equality relates a squared derivative of the recursion to the breakpoint itself.

Near the minimizing breakpoint, a Taylor estimate gives a lower bound on the curvature of the inserted-mass variation.

## 5. The quadratic overlap penalty

<!-- passage:sec-5 -->

For overlaps sufficiently close to the target breakpoint, the second interpolation gives a quadratic pressure deficit with uniform constants.

**Proposition 5.7. Strict gap beyond neighboring breakpoints.** Outside the two neighboring breakpoints, the interleaved recursion gives a strict pressure gap below $2\psi(t)$.

Combine the quadratic estimate near the breakpoint with a uniform deficit away from it. On the bounded overlap interval the latter also yields a quadratic bound.

When $q_{r-1}\le u\le q_r$, integrate the derivative bound in the second interpolation and compare its decoupled endpoint with the scalar recursion.

When $q_r\le u\le q_{r+1}$, insert the overlap to the right of the target breakpoint. The mass and covariance increments change with that placement.

In the interleaved construction, $|u|$ is itself a breakpoint of the second interpolation. The estimate compares the new zero endpoint with the original constrained pressure.

Interchanging two adjacent Gaussian operators increases their composition when the larger mass goes on the inside. For the strictly convex functions obtained from the Parisi recursion, positive variances and distinct masses give a strict inequality.
